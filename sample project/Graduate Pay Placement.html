How this was built

A public file nobody uses,
turned operational.

The survey publishes quartiles, not people. Privacy law blocks the raw records. So the first job was reconstructing the population — then testing whether anything in it was actually predictable.

01

Reconstruct

4,340 graduate profiles sampled from log-normal distributions fitted to each programme's published 25th, 50th and 75th percentiles. Placement drawn from published employment rates.

02

Explore

Distribution shape, faculty rankings, quartile spreads, the gap between mean and median. Three findings surfaced here that the headline numbers hide entirely.

03

Model

Four regressors on salary, four classifiers on placement. 80/20 split, fixed seed, held-out evaluation. Best selected on test performance, not training fit.

04

Interrogate

Feature importance, residual analysis, and a confusion matrix that exposed the classifier as far weaker than its accuracy score suggested.

Finding one · the shape

The graduate market is not
one market. It is two.

Plot every salaried graduate on one axis and the distribution has a long right shoulder. Split it by field and the reason is obvious — Computing and Arts barely overlap.

Computing

Sits almost entirely above S$5,500. The distribution has its own centre of gravity — this is not a tail of the main curve, it is a separate population.

Arts & Social Sciences

Concentrated between S$3,800 and S$5,200. Overlap with Computing is minimal — the two groups are competing in different labour markets entirely.

What it means

Any single "fresh graduate benchmark" averages across two distributions that share almost no ground. It will be wrong for both.

Finding one · the ranking

The market, in order.

Computing owns the top

Four of the top five programmes come from one school. Law is the only non-technical degree above S$6,000 — and it reports a year later than everyone else, after training.

Curriculum, not cohort

Data Science & Analytics and Business Analytics both clear S$5,600 against general Science below S$4,700. Same faculties, same admissions bar. The return is on what is taught.

The floor

Top to bottom is S$3,611 a month — more than the entire salary of the graduate at the bottom of the table.

Finding two · the trap

The number every salary survey
quotes is the wrong one.

Benchmarks report the mean. For programmes with a long right tail, the mean describes a graduate who barely exists in the cohort.

57.4% of Business Administration graduates earn less than their own programme's mean. A small group entering banking and consulting drags the average S$329 above the median. Quote the mean in an offer conversation and six in ten candidates will never reach the figure you named.

Quartile spread — widest and narrowest programmes

Wide spread means the degree sets a range, not a rate. BBA graduates span S$3,782 to S$6,585 between quartiles — outcome depends on the path taken after graduation. Nursing spans a few hundred dollars: a regulated scale, entirely predictable.

Finding three · the map

Everybody is recruiting
in the same corner.

Placement rate is read as a quality ranking. Plotted against salary it becomes something more useful — a map of where the competition is, and where it is not.

Top right · contested

Computer Science, Information Systems, Law. Placement near 90%, salaries at the ceiling. Every campus recruiter is here. By the time a mid-sized employer arrives the cohort is gone and the price has moved.

Bottom left · overlooked

Biomedical Engineering at 55.0% placed. Industrial Design at 61.7%. Same quantitative and design training as their placed peers — a third to a half still in market at six months.

The uncomfortable one

Nursing and Accountancy sit top-left on placement, bottom on pay. Near-total employment has not moved salaries at all. Demand without pricing power.

Finding four · the headline

It is not the GPA.
It never was.

We asked the model which piece of information about a graduate mattered most for predicting salary. The answer inverts how nearly every graduate scheme screens applications.

Relative feature importance, Random Forest regressor. Field and faculty together account for 70.3% of explained variance — 3.3× the contribution of GPA.

The screening error

A blanket "3.5 minimum" filters on a variable worth 21.0% of the outcome while ignoring the one worth 70.3%. It also cuts hardest into faculties that grade harshest — removing exactly the candidates whose degree predicts strong pay.

Correlation check

GPA correlates with salary at just 0.086; internship count at 0.219. Both real, both small. Neither is close to what the degree itself carries.

Honours, 2.2%

The weakest predictor in the set. It functions as an admission signal, not a pay signal — and is priced as if it were the reverse.

The models · salary

Predicting pay to ±S$630.

Four regressors, held-out test set. The simplest model won — which is itself the finding.

R² on held-out data

Linear regression beat all three tree ensembles. The relationship between degree and pay is close to additive — each faculty carries a fixed premium. There is no hidden interaction for a boosted model to find, and the complexity buys nothing.

Actual vs predicted

Gold line = perfect prediction. Tight around the diagonal in the mid range, wider at both extremes.

Error distribution
R² 0.543

Linear Regression · RMSE S$880 · MAE S$630. Errors centre on zero with no systematic bias — the model is not consistently over- or under-shooting any group.

The models · placement

An 81.6% accurate model
that is nearly useless.

This is the result we almost reported as a success. The accuracy score looked strong. The confusion matrix showed what it was actually doing.

ROC curve

Genuine signal above chance — the model has learnt something real.

Confusion matrix

It predicts "placed" for almost everyone.

The trap

80.9% of graduates are placed. A model that answers "placed" every single time scores 80.9% accuracy and has learnt nothing. Ours scored 81.6% — barely above doing no work at all.

What it actually does

Recall 98.7% — it catches nearly every placed graduate. Specificity 9.0% — it catches almost none of the unplaced ones. On the only question worth asking, who is still available, it fails.

Why report it

AUC 0.731 says there is real signal in the features. The failure is not the algorithm — it is that placement is driven by variables the survey never collects. That is a data problem, and naming it is more useful than a flattering number.

What follows from this

Five decisions that should
change on Monday.

01 · Compensation

Retire the single graduate band

Between-faculty spread exceeds S$3,000. Within most faculties it is under S$900. One company-wide band averages across the largest source of variance in the data and lands wrong on both ends.

02 · Benchmarking

Quote medians for business roles

The BBA mean runs S$329 above the median. 57.4% of the cohort sits below it. Anchoring an offer to the mean sets an expectation most hires will never meet.

03 · Sourcing

Add three faculties to the circuit

Biomedical Engineering, Industrial Design and Environmental Studies each carry 38–46% unplaced graduates at six months, with quantitative and design training that transfers. No bidding war attached.

04 · Screening

Drop the blanket GPA cut-off

It screens on 21.0% of the signal, discards candidates from harshly-graded faculties, and correlates with salary at only 0.086. Screen on degree relevance, then assess directly.

05 · Reporting

Publish intervals, not point estimates

Twelve programmes in this survey report under thirty respondents. A single median drawn from eighteen people is presented to eighteen-year-olds as fact, and it steers a decision worth 2.01× over a career.

The gap worth closing

Internship count explains 6.4% of salary. That strongly suggests internship quality — where, doing what, converting to an offer or not — is the variable that matters, and it appears nowhere in the survey. Collecting it would likely fix the placement classifier too.

Predictive Workforce Analytics — Graduate Pay & Placement
Full version · project section or LinkedIn

Built an end-to-end predictive workforce analytics pipeline in PySpark on 4,340 graduate profiles reconstructed from Singapore's national Graduate Employment Survey, training 8 models across salary regression and placement classification. Established that degree choice explains 70.3% of graduate salary variance — 3.3x the contribution of GPA — evidencing a shift from single company-wide graduate pay bands to faculty-segmented structures, and challenging the blanket GPA cut-off used across graduate recruitment. Best regressor predicted salary to S$630 mean absolute error (R² 0.543). Identified a mean-versus-median distortion inflating reported business-graduate pay by S$329/month, and mapped three under-recruited faculties holding 38-46% unplaced qualified graduates. Diagnosed the placement classifier's 81.6% accuracy as a class-imbalance artefact against an 80.9% no-skill baseline, isolating internship quality as the missing predictor.

Answering the four questions you will get
  • "Walk me through the tech." PySpark, ML Pipeline API, 8 models, 80/20 held-out split. Linear regression won — the relationship is additive, ensembles bought nothing.
  • "What surprised you?" Lead with the classifier failure. An 81.6% accuracy score against an 80.9% no-skill baseline. Catching that is the finding.
  • "Your data is synthetic." Say so first, unprompted. Reconstructed from published quartiles because privacy law blocks individual records — standard practice, and the aggregate statistics are real.
  • "What next?" Internship quality. Count explains 6.4%; quality is uncollected and almost certainly where the missing placement signal lives.
