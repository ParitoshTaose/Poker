The Ecosystem Engine

An online poker company does not gamble. It takes a tiny cut of every pot — so it only makes money if people keep playing. The strong players slowly take all the money from the casual players, the casual players quit, and then the whole business dies. This project builds the data pipeline and the machine-learning models that let management see that happening — and stop it — using 21.6 million real hands of real-money online poker.

01 Start here

Poker in 60 seconds — the business, not the game

You do not need to know how to play poker to run this project. You need to understand where the money goes. That is all.

Six people sit at a table. Each is dealt two secret cards. They bet. Five shared cards are turned over one group at a time, with more betting in between. Whoever has the best five-card hand at the end takes all the money in the middle — the pot. Then the next hand starts, and it all happens again, roughly every 30 seconds.

Here is the part that matters for us. The poker company is not one of the six players. It does not win or lose. It simply takes a small percentage out of every pot — usually about 5%, with a cap of a dollar or two. That cut is called the rake. It is the company's entire revenue.

Key idea — the one formula Revenue = number of hands played × average rake per hand. The company does not care who wins. It cares only that hands keep being played. Every business question in this project comes back to protecting that number.

The four words you need

WordWhat it means in plain English
RakeThe company's cut of each pot. About 5%, capped. This is the revenue.
Big blind (bb)A forced bet that starts every hand, so there is always something to fight over. It is also the unit of measurement in poker. At a "25NL" table the big blind is $0.25; at "1000NL" it is $10. Saying "she won 40 big blinds" means the same thing at both tables — saying "she won $10" does not.
Recreational playerSomeone playing for fun, usually on one table, in the evening, who loses slowly over time. Also called a "fish". They are the customer. All the money in the system comes from them.
Regular / grinderSomeone playing for profit, often on 8–24 tables at once, for hours. Also called a "shark" or "reg". They win money from the recreational players.
Takeaway Poker between players is a zero-sum game — for someone to win a rupee, someone else must lose it. But the company's business is not zero-sum: it wants the same money to circulate round and round for as long as possible, taking a small slice each lap. Its enemy is not losing — its enemy is the game stopping.
02 Act I · The business problem

The lake is dying, and nobody can see it

This is the real strategic problem of every online poker company on earth. It has a name in the industry: ecosystem management.

The fishing lake analogy

Imagine you own a fishing lake. You do not fish. You make money by selling fishing licences. There are fish in the lake, and there are sharks — very good anglers who catch a lot.

If the sharks catch fish at a sensible rate, everything is fine. The fish enjoy their day out, come back next weekend, and you sell licences forever. But if you let in too many sharks, or let them fish twelve rods at once, the fish get cleaned out in a single afternoon. They go home, they do not come back, and within a season your lake is nothing but sharks staring at each other in empty water. Then the sharks leave too — because there is nothing left to catch. Your licence revenue goes to zero.

That is exactly an online poker room. The recreational players are the fish. The grinders are the sharks. The rake is your licence fee. And the disaster is slow, quiet, and completely invisible on a normal revenue dashboard — because revenue looks great right up until the moment it collapses.

A reinforcing feedback loop — the exact thing Big Data is supposed to catch early. By the time it shows up in monthly revenue, it is far too late to fix.

So what are we actually asked to solve?

The project guidelines say the team must answer three executive questions. Here they are, answered for this project:

The questionOur answer
What business problem are we solving? Our most valuable customers — recreational players — are being drained by a small group of professional players, and we cannot see it happening until they have already gone. We need an early-warning system.
How did we engineer the pipeline to solve it at scale? We ingest 21.6 million raw hand-history records, explode them into roughly 229 million individual player actions, and use distributed computing to turn that into one clean row per player with ~25 engineered behaviour columns.
What should management do? Four specific, costed interventions: table caps, seating rules, targeted retention offers, and a restructured loyalty scheme — each aimed at a segment the model identifies.
Takeaway This is a customer-retention and ecosystem-health problem that happens to be set in a poker company. Swap "poker room" for "marketplace" and "grinder" for "power seller who undercuts everyone", and it is the same problem Amazon, Uber and Zomato all have. That is why it interviews well.
03 Act I · Justifying the "Big" in Big Data

Why a spreadsheet genuinely cannot do this

The guidelines demand a problem "where spreadsheet-based analysis is no longer practical." Most student projects just assert that. We can prove it with arithmetic.

Here is the key insight that makes this project big: one hand of poker is not one row of data. A single hand contains a whole sequence of decisions — every player's fold, check, call, bet and raise, on each of four betting rounds. To analyse behaviour you need every one of those decisions as its own row.

The fan-out. This multiplication is what turns a manageable file into a genuine distributed-computing problem. Both averages were measured from the real data.

Key idea — the slide that wins the argument Microsoft Excel stops at 1,048,576 rows. That is a hard technical ceiling, not a preference. Our action-level table is roughly 229 million rows — about 218 times over the limit. We could not open 1/200th of our data in a spreadsheet. And it is not just size. To compute one player's statistics you must scan every action they ever took, scattered across thousands of files. That is a group-by across the entire dataset — exactly the operation Spark exists for.

There is a second, subtler reason this needs distributed computing. Our most powerful feature — how many tables was this player sitting at simultaneously? — cannot be read off any single row. It requires taking every hand, grouping by player and by minute, and counting distinct tables. That is a shuffle across the whole dataset. On one laptop it is hopeless; on a cluster it is routine.

Takeaway Our "why Big Data" answer is not "the file is large". It is "the unit of analysis is a decision, there are 229 million of them, and the feature that matters most can only be computed by reshuffling all of them at once." That sentence alone is worth marks.
04 Act II · The data

Where the data comes from — and it is real

This is the question that kills most project ideas. For this one, the answer is unusually good: a properly published, openly licensed, academically citable dataset of real money games.

The source

The dataset is "A Dataset of Poker Hand Histories", maintained by the Computer Poker Research Group at the University of Toronto. It is published two ways — on GitHub for browsing, and on Zenodo (an academic data repository run by CERN) with a permanent DOI for citing.

Key idea — use only ONE part of it The 20.3 GB archive contains two very different things. Take the second, ignore the first.

What is inside the real-money portion

These are anonymised hand histories collected from six commercial poker sites between 1 July and 23 July 2009, at stakes from 25NL (25¢ big blind — casual money) up to 1000NL ($10 big blind — serious money). They were originally published by a company called HandHQ and have been converted into the standard PHH format.

No-limit Texas hold'em — the standard game. Every hand in our subset is this, so we can ignore it.
blinds_or_straddles = [0.10, 0.25, 0, 0]
The forced bets. The big blind is $0.25, so this is a 25NL table. This is the number we divide all money by to make cheap and expensive tables comparable.

How much money each player sat down with. Useful: casual players often buy in for an odd, small amount; professionals almost always buy in for the exact maximum.
'd dh p1 ????'

'p3 f'
Player 3 folds — gives up the hand. This is the single most common action in poker.
'p4 cbr 0.75'
cbr = complete / bet / raise. Player 4 raises to $0.75. In big blinds, that is 3bb — a standard opening raise.
'p2 cc'
cc = check or call. Either "I'll match that bet" or "I bet nothing but stay in".
'd db 4dQs4s'
db = deal board. The three shared cards are turned over: four of diamonds, queen of spades, four of spades.

07 Act III · The build

The architecture, in three layers

The guidelines want a diagram that goes Business Problem → Data Sources → Ingestion → Storage → Engineering → Features → ML → Insights → Recommendations. Here is ours, using the standard industry pattern.

Professional data teams almost always use a three-layer design nicknamed medallion architecture — Bronze, Silver, Gold. The idea is simple: never destroy the original, and never let the messy version touch the model. Each layer is cleaner and smaller than the last.

Data shrinks as it rises: 20 GB of text → 229M structured rows → a few hundred thousand player rows. Only the top layer touches the models.
Key idea — why keep Bronze at all? Because you will get the parser wrong the first time. If you overwrite the raw files while "cleaning" them, a bug means re-downloading 20 GB. Keeping Bronze untouched means every mistake costs a re-run, not a re-download. Say this in the report — it is exactly the kind of engineering judgement the 4-mark "Data Engineering & Architecture" criterion is looking for.

Where it all runs

The guidelines specify Databricks and PySpark for Phases 2 and 3. Note one practical thing: the old free "Databricks Community Edition" was retired on 1 January 2026. The replacement is Databricks Free Edition — still free, and it no longer requires an AWS or Azure account to sign up. Confirm with the professor which one the course expects.

08 Phase 1 · Sessions 1–9

Getting the data in and storing it properly

Phase 1 is about acquisition and storage — and the marks come from explaining your choices, not from the download itself.

  1. Acquire. Pull the archive from Zenodo (DOI 10.5281/zenodo.17136841). Start with a single site-and-stake folder to develop against; add the rest once the parser is stable.
  2. Land it as Bronze. Copy the .phhs files, untouched, into distributed storage. Folder path carries the metadata: /bronze/handhq/venue=PS/stake=0.25/.
  3. Partition deliberately. Split by venue and day. This means a query about PokerStars on 3 July reads only that slice instead of scanning 20 GB — the storage equivalent of a good index.
  4. Convert to Parquet at the Silver layer. Parquet is columnar: it stores each column separately, so a query that needs only player_id and action never reads the card columns. Typically both far smaller and far faster than raw text.
  5. Document the scalability story. Explain what would change at 100× the data — more partitions, more worker nodes, and (if this were live) Kafka streaming the actions in real time instead of a nightly batch.
Takeaway — how to earn the Phase 1 marks Do not just write "we stored the data in HDFS". Write why: why Bronze/Silver/Gold, why partition by venue and day, why Parquet instead of CSV, and what you would add if this were a live system rather than a 23-day archive. Three sentences of justification beat three screenshots.
Worth raising with the professor The course teaches an HDFS simulation (the cluster/ folder from Session 1 and 4). Ask whether Phase 1 should demonstrate real distributed storage on Databricks, or whether replicating the taught simulation on this dataset is expected. This is a good, specific question for your 10:00 AM slot — it shows you have already read the guidelines.

This is the heart of the project. The raw data contains almost nothing you can model directly — every useful column has to be built. That is the point.

Step 2 — Clean, and justify every choice

Key idea — the multi-tabling feature is your showpiece Every other feature is a simple count. "How many tables was this player on at the same moment?" is not — it requires joining a player's actions to themselves across tables, bucketed by time. It is the one feature that genuinely needs a distributed engine, it is the single best casual-vs-professional signal, and a non-technical audience understands it instantly. Put it on a slide.

Step 4 — Encode and scale

Two final mechanical steps the guidelines explicitly ask for. Text columns like venue become numbers (StringIndexer then OneHotEncoder). And every numeric feature gets standardised — rescaled so they are all on a comparable range.

Why scaling is not optional here: K-Means measures straight-line distance between players. If "hands played" runs 0–50,000 and "VPIP" runs 0–1, the distance calculation is entirely dominated by hands played and VPIP is effectively ignored. Scaling puts them on equal footing. That sentence is a mark.

10 Phase 3 · Sessions 14–18

The three models

The guidelines require at least two analytical approaches from PySpark MLlib. We propose three, which map onto three genuine business questions.

Model 1 — Who is who? (Clustering · K-Means)

We do not have labels saying "this one is a professional". We have to discover the groups from behaviour. K-Means takes every player's feature row and sorts them into k groups such that players inside a group behave similarly.

Choose k using the silhouette score (a number saying how cleanly separated the groups are) plus the elbow method, and — crucially — whether the groups make sense to a human.

What we expect the clusters to look like
Click a segment. Each dot is a player: across = how many hands they play, up = how aggressive they are.
Four groups we expect the model to find. These are hypotheses drawn from how poker actually works — not results. The real clusters come out of the data, and part of the analysis is checking whether they match this picture. Tip: the two axes shown are only 2 of ~25 features. The real model clusters in all of them at once.
Where it breaks — do not oversell this Those four names are what the poker world expects, not what your model has found. When you run it, some clusters will be messier, and you may get five or three instead of four. Report what actually comes out, including any cluster you cannot explain. Examiners reward honesty about a messy cluster far more than a suspiciously tidy story.

Model 2 — Who is about to leave? (Classification)

Now the money question. Here is the neat part: we do not need anyone to label the data for us — the answer is already sitting in the timestamps. We split the 23 days in two:

Features come from the first window; the label comes from the second. The label is free and honest — nobody had to guess it.

Then train and compare, exactly as the guidelines ask:

  • Logistic Regression — the simple, explainable baseline. Always start here.
  • Random Forest — usually more accurate; also gives feature importances, so you can tell management which behaviours predict leaving.
  • Gradient Boosted Trees — often the strongest, but hardest to explain.

Judge them on AUC, precision, recall and a confusion matrix — not accuracy. If 80% of players churn, a model that predicts "everyone churns" is 80% accurate and completely worthless. Saying this out loud earns the "Model Evaluation & Interpretation" marks.

Key idea — pick the metric that matches the money A false alarm costs you one unnecessary bonus offer. A miss costs you a customer forever. Those are not equal, so recall matters more than precision here, and you should say so and choose your decision threshold accordingly. That is analytics translated into a business decision — which is exactly the skill being marked.

Model 3 — What is a player worth? (Regression)

Predict how much rake a player will generate in the outcome window, using Linear Regression versus a Random Forest Regressor, judged on RMSE and R². Combined with Model 2 you get the number management actually wants: expected revenue at risk = probability of leaving × their value.

Takeaway Two models satisfy the requirement; three tell a complete story — who they are, whether they are leaving, and how much that costs. If time runs short, drop Model 3 and keep 1 and 2.
11 Act IV · The payoff

What management actually does on Monday morning

Models do not earn marks. Decisions do. The rubric gives 2 marks for recommendations and 3 for executive storytelling — that is a quarter of the grade riding on this section alone.

FindingRecommendationWhy it works
A small group of players sits at 12+ tables and wins steadily Cap simultaneous tables (e.g. 6) and ban automatic seating scripts. Slows extraction without banning anyone. Real operators have done exactly this.
Some tables are 5 grinders and 1 recreational Change seating rules so recreational players are not seated into ambushes. Directly extends the life of your most valuable customers.
The model flags casual players at high risk of leaving Trigger a retention offer — a deposit match or a free tournament ticket — before they go. Cheaper to keep a player than acquire one. The model tells you exactly who to spend on.
Loyalty rewards flow to the biggest extractors Restructure the scheme to reward frequency and loyalty, not volume alone. Stops the business from subsidising the very behaviour that is killing it.
A cluster shows very long sessions and rising losses Responsible-gaming intervention — session reminders, deposit-limit prompts. Ethically right, increasingly a regulatory requirement, and it protects long-term revenue.
Key idea — quantify at least one of them Do not stop at "we recommend capping tables". Compute the number: "the top 5% of players by extraction rate take X big blinds per day out of the recreational pool; if the churn model is right, that costs roughly Y% of monthly rake." One real number, computed from your own pipeline, is worth more than five well-phrased suggestions.
The framing to lead with Present this as protecting customers and the long-term health of the business, not as extracting more from gamblers. It is both the honest reading and the stronger one — and it makes the responsible-gaming recommendation land as a genuine strategic insight rather than a box-ticking afterthought.

The data is from July 2009 It is 17 years old. Games were softer then and the industry has changed. State it on the dataset slide. Argue that the method is what transfers, and the ecosystem dynamic is unchanged. Do not hide the date.
Rake is not in the data The company's actual revenue per hand was never recorded. Derive it as money-in minus money-out, validate on a sample, and label it clearly as an estimate throughout.
Only 23 days Real churn is measured over months. Ours is a 9-day window. Call it "short-horizon lapse", not "churn". Precision in wording is itself a mark.
No ground truth for bots Nobody labelled which accounts were cheating, so supervised bot detection is impossible. This is why we chose churn as the label. If you want a bot angle, frame it as unsupervised anomaly detection and claim nothing more.
Players appear on one site only Someone playing on both PokerStars and PartyPoker looks like two different people. Analyse each platform separately. Never merge player IDs across venues.
Free-tier compute limits 229M rows on a free Databricks cluster will be slow. Develop on one site + one stake. Run the full set once, at the end, and screenshot it.
Everyone will use Kaggle Not really a risk — an advantage. A Zenodo DOI, a real licence and an IEEE citation make your data section look like research, not a download.
Where it breaks — the one that could sink you Check the professor is comfortable with a gambling-industry topic before you invest three weeks. The guidelines list open domains and do not exclude it, and the responsible-gaming angle is a strong defence — but this is a 30-second question at an office-hours slot, and the answer changes everything. Ask on day one.
