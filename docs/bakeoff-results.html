NMIMS · MBA Business Analytics · Trimester IV · Step 5 · Fork bake-off

Five questions, one survives

The roadmap's Step 5 said: prototype the candidate ML questions cheaply, score each against a dumb baseline, and carry one winner into the expensive phases. This page is what came back. Fifteen models, 41 seconds, on the real Gold tables — and the cleanest fork on paper turned out to have nothing to predict.

Every candidate was scored against its own stated baseline — majority-class for the classifier, a per-venue mean for the player regressions, a per-stake mean for the hand regression. A score with no baseline beside it is not evidence.

01 Read first

Five candidate questions, each trained on the same Gold tables, each scored against its own dumb baseline, and each given two algorithms — a linear model and a gradient-boosted tree. Fifteen model fits in 41 seconds — and that speed is the architecture working, not a shortcut. 116.6 million seat-rows went into the medallion pipeline and 18 MB came out. Key idea — why "skill", not R²
Skill = 1 − SSE(model) ⁄ SSE(baseline). Plain R² measures a model against the global mean. But when the honest baseline is already a per-venue or per-stake mean — and it should be, because stakes and venues differ enormously — R² quietly credits the model for knowing something the baseline already knew. Skill compares like with like. A skill score at or below zero means the model adds nothing.
03 The finding

The leakage ablation — why the two biggest numbers can't be trusted

Every rate column in Gold — vpip, pfr, fold_rate, wtsd, aggression_factor — is averaged over a player's entire recorded history, final week included. So a model predicting the final week from those rates is partly reading its own answer. The fork specs flagged this on paper. This run measured it.

The test is simple: fit the same gradient-boosted tree three times, on three feature sets — only the features that are honestly known before the cutoff; only the lifetime rates; and both together.

The same pattern holds for Fork B's volume variant: +0.284 prior-window only, +0.320 lifetime rates only, +0.470 both.
Where it breaks
Both forward-looking forks are quoting an optimistic ceiling. Closing this properly means extending features.py to compute rate columns restricted to day <= cutoff — one extra Spark pass, the same shape of work already done twice on this project. Until that exists, Fork A's 0.927 and Fork B volume's +0.470 are not defensible in a viva, and an examiner who asks "are those averages computed over the period you're predicting?" will land the blow.
Takeaway
Fork B's money variant is the only supervised candidate this does not touch. It describes value realised over a player's whole history and makes no forward-looking claim — so lifetime aggregation is the correct treatment there, not a defect. Its +0.224 needs no asterisk. That single fact is worth more to the fork decision than any accuracy number on this page.
04 Verdict

Fork A · lapse classification — 17, the measured winner

Flag players about to go quiet, before their data stops. The best combination of coverage and operator action: 98,718 players across all six venues, and the output is a retention list a team can literally work through on a Monday morning.

Verifiedclass balance — previously unmeasured
62.5% lapsed — 61,702 of 98,718 players with hands_prior > 0. The fork specs listed this as an open decision ("do not assume it's balanced, and do not guess a number for the report"). 06 Verdict

Fork B · volume — 13, the highest score and the least trustworthy

Predict next-week volume (hands_final7) instead of money. It posted the highest raw skill of any candidate (+0.427) — and it is the candidate the ablation hits hardest.

Where it breaks — the target is zero most of the time
62.5% of players have hands_final7 = 0. Median 0, mean 195, max 39,272. This is a classification problem wearing a regression's clothes: most of the "prediction" is really deciding whether someone shows up at all. It needed a log1p transform just to stop a handful of whales from dictating the entire error — on the raw scale, an unclipped Ridge prediction expands to 283,686 hands and single-handedly decides the RMSE.
Takeaway
It loses to its own money sibling, which asks a similar business question without the asterisk. Where it does earn its keep is as the report's cleanest illustration of why we checked — the fork with the best-looking number was the one with the worst-founded number.

Predict pot size in big blinds from conditions known before a card is dealt — stake, seat count, hour, venue. On paper this was the cleanest fork of the three: no leakage, ~100% coverage, one row per hand across 21.5 million hands. It has no signal at all.

Key idea — why this was always going to fail, in hindsight
Pot size is decided by the cards that come out and the decisions players make in response to them. None of that is knowable before the deal. Day, hour, seat count and stake describe the container, not the contents. The one feature that would carry real information — who is sitting at the table — is exactly the feature the fork excluded to stay leakage-free.
Takeaway — this is a result, not a failure
Put this in the report. "The methodologically cleanest of our three candidate questions turned out to have nothing to predict, and we found that out in an afternoon rather than in week three" is exactly what Step 5 was for, and it is a far better answer to justify every decision than any accuracy number. A negative result you went looking for is evidence of method.

Where it breaks — the clusters are gradients, not groups
A silhouette of 0.279 is weak separation. These are not four natural species of player; they are four convenient cuts through a continuum, and the score falling as k rises says the data has no strong natural k. That is normal for behavioural data and it is fine — but do not present the segments as if the data discovered them. Present them as a chosen, business-readable partition, and say the silhouette out loud.
09 Defects

Two bugs this run found

Both were invisible to every check upstream. Both are the same lesson the uncalled-bet bug taught in Step 4: a downstream check finds upstream bugs that every upstream check passed.

1 — iPoker records no starting stacks, and it arrives as inf, not as null
The raw .phhs files contain the literal starting_stacks = [inf, inf]. inf is a valid TOML float, so tomllib parsed it silently, nothing upstream ever raised, and it reached Gold intact: avg_stack_bb is +inf on all 15,549 iPoker players and all 5,996,194 iPoker hands (min_stack_bb too). It detonates StandardScaler, Ridge and every distance metric on contact — which is how it was found.

This is the third iPoker-shaped hole, after no table identity and no reconciling winnings: iPoker is behaviourally complete and economically blind. The fix belongs in features.py, beside the tables_recorded flag it exactly parallels — null the column, carry a stacks_recorded boolean. Never impute it: a median stack on a venue that recorded none is the same class of lie as max_tables = 1.
2 — hands_per_day is an algebraic leak, not a soft one
It is hands_played / days_active, and hands_played = hands_prior + hands_final7. With hands_prior also in the feature set, a model recovers the label outright: 
hands_final7 = hands_per_day × days_active − hands_prior
The first pass of this script scored Fork A at 0.960 PR-AUC on exactly that. It is now in FORWARD_LEAKS alongside money_coverage, which leaks the same denominator. Both remain legal for the money variant, which is not forward-looking.
Takeaway
Neither bug was found by reading code. Both were found because a number came back too good — 0.960 where 0.80 was plausible, and a crash where a scaler should have worked. Build the check that makes implausible results loud, then believe it when it fires.
10 Decide

The open decision

The bake-off has done its job: it eliminated Fork C on evidence, exposed a leak that would have inflated two headline numbers all the way to the viva, and left two defensible candidates. The remaining choice is a genuine trade-off, and it belongs to the team.

Recommendation, and the condition on it
Fork A as the spine, K-Means as the unsupervised base layer — it wins the scorecard, keeps all six venues, and its story needs no reframing. Conditional on extending features.py with prior-window-only rate columns before Step 8; without that, the headline metric is not defensible.

Choose Fork B (money) instead if the team would rather not spend that extension, or wants the money story specifically. It is a legitimate winner on the cleanest-methodology axis and needs no new Gold columns.
Takeaway — the loser is not wasted
Both models already exist in src/bakeoff.py. Whichever fork loses becomes the report's alternatives considered paragraph with real measured numbers behind it, rather than a hand-wave — which is "justify every decision" in its purest form, and rubric material twice over.

Step 5 is done the moment the winner is written down. Three things then follow, in order: finalise docs/bakeoff-decision.md with the pick (this clears Gate 0.3), patch features.py for the inf defect (worth doing whichever fork wins), and add both defects to CLAUDE.md's gotchas so they are never re-derived.
