The business problem

The house never wins a hand.
It rents the seat — and the tenants are leaving.

A poker operator has no stake in who wins. It takes roughly a twentieth of each pot — measured across all six venues in this data, the median rake is 4.3%–5.0% of the pot, and exactly $0.00 when no flop is dealt. Revenue is therefore a pure function of how many hands get played, which is a pure function of how many losing players are willing to keep sitting down.

The question this project answers

Which players will stop playing next week — and how much of next week's rake leaves with them?

Both halves matter. A churn score alone cannot tell an operator whether the player about to leave was worth $13 a week or $221. The deliverable is one ranked, named, exportable list — not a metric.

Why this is a retention problem, not a poker problem

Nothing here models how to win at poker, and nothing identifies a player to exploit. The same list that protects revenue is the list a responsible-gaming team would want: it flags the customers who are losing fastest and disengaging quietest. We rank who to contact and protect, never who to target.

The test that caught a real bug

Poker is zero-sum apart from the rake, so Σ net_bb must equal −Σ rake_bb. Measured at full scale: −2,064,330 vs −2,064,329, ratio 1.0000. Before the uncalled-bet fix this identity failed on 99.4% of hands.

Local Spark (local[8], 9 GB driver) on one laptop; the logic re-hosts to Databricks unchanged.

Why the compression is the argument

Excel stops at 1,048,576 rows. The seat table is 111× that and the action table 175×. But the table the models read is 18 MB — the distributed work happens once, in feature engineering, and never again.

Schema-on-read, honestly

The same field arrives as int in one hand and float in the next, and one venue writes string hand IDs. Types are declared explicitly rather than inferred — inference killed two full runs before that changed.

What we refused to impute

One venue writes the vendor's name into every table field, so its multi-tabling count is a missing value wearing a plausible number. It is nulled, not filled with 1 — filling it would file 13,201 professionals as single-table recreational players.

The two that pay, leave

Recreational and Gambler are 60.9% of players but 65.9% of everyone who goes quiet — and they hold 56% of the rake at risk ($340,379).

The one that doesn't, stays

The Grinder loses least — -3.3 bb/100, essentially just the rake — and is the stickiest player on the platform. Per head a Grinder pays $220.88 of weekly rake against a Recreational player's $13.59. That measures who paid it, not whose money funded it.

Why multi-tabling earns its place

On betting style alone no professional cluster forms at all — two of four clusters both land on “recreational”. Style cannot separate a professional from a customer; table count can. That single feature is why the segmentation is usable.

What we will not oversell

These are gradients, not species. Silhouette actually peaks at k=2 (0.439); k=4 costs about 0.12 and was chosen for business readability. Bisecting k-means scores better (0.319 vs 0.228) and the two algorithms place only 67% of players in the same group. Two of our four hypothesised archetypes were also wrong: no ultra-tight “nit” cluster exists, and the loose cluster is passive, not aggressive. We report the misses.

Finding two · supervised · short-horizon lapse

Going quiet is predictable a week ahead —
from behaviour recorded before the week starts.

Label: zero hands in the next seven days, measured against each venue's own last recorded day, because the six venues do not share a calendar. Every feature is recomputed from hands on or before the cutoff, so the model structurally cannot see the week it is predicting.

The finding that changed the population

The obvious filter — “players with at least 100 hands” — counts hands over a player's whole history, including the week being predicted. Someone with 30 prior hands only clears it by playing 70 more in the label week, which means by definitely not lapsing.

The arithmetic is decisive: the lifetime rule keeps 98,718 players, the prior-window rule keeps 90,469, and both contain exactly 61,702 lapsers. Every one of the 8,249 extra players is a non-lapser admitted by a filter that had already seen the answer. Prevalence moves from 62.5% to 68.2% once it is fixed.

The leakage check, re-run on the honest features

Same estimator, same players, same split. Volume and tenure alone: 0.690. Lifetime rates, which can see the label week: 0.819. Prior-window only: 0.857. The honest feature set wins — the earlier ordering had been the other way round, which is the signature of leakage.

What the model is given · 30 prior-window features
  • Recency & rhythm — days since last hand, share of tenure days actually played, minutes at the table, hands per minute.
  • Decay — hands in the last week against the week before, and the share of all volume falling in the final three days.
  • Style — VPIP, pre-flop raise, aggression factor, showdown rate, average investment per hand.
  • Context — stakes played and their spread, seats per table, button share, and a dummy per venue.

Recency is the strongest single feature (GBT importance 0.249) and the next is worth a third as much. We say so rather than overselling the behavioural columns.

Where it nearly failed

A four-minute spreadsheet sort scores 0.800.
At its default setting, our model loses to it.

Sorting players by days-since-last-seen needs no model and no training. It is the bar every line of this pipeline has to clear, so it is on the slide rather than in an appendix.

The honest loss

At the default 0.5 cut-point, the rule “quiet for a day” scores 0.856 on F1 and our GBT scores 0.829. On a 68/32 problem, 0.5 is an inherited default, not a decision. Chosen deliberately, GBT reaches 0.867 at a threshold of 0.40.

Where the model does earn its place

Under a real contact budget. At 2,260 calls the model finds 2,192 true lapsers against the free sort's 2,001 — 191 fewer offers posted to people who were never leaving, every week.

What we are not claiming

The split is by player, never by period, so no temporal backtest exists and we do not claim the model generalises to a future month. It is also weakest where lapsing is rarest (0.769 on Ongame against 0.861 on PokerStars). Both are named limitations, not discovered ones.

Twenty-six days of data cannot show that anyone left for good. Everything here is short-horizon lapse — a week of silence, an early warning — and it is called that throughout.

Finding three · attaching money to the score

The model ranks players. It does not price them —
and both halves of the multiplication were broken.

Defect 1 · ranked right, priced wrong

The classifier was fitted with balanced class weights, which drags every predicted probability toward 0.5. ROC-AUC cannot see it — it only reads order — but a dollar figure multiplies by that number directly.

At 68/32 there was never an imbalance to correct. Removing the weights moved the worst decile from 0.200 off to 0.026 while ROC-AUC did not move (0.857 to 0.857). A 0.03 tolerance was written into the code before the run, so no calibration layer was needed.

Defect 2 · rake is derived, and the hands that reconcile are the big ones

Rake is not a recorded column anywhere in this data. It is pot − paid-out winnings, and on venues whose exports are incomplete that residual only looks sane on large pots. On PartyPoker the hands where it reconciles have a median pot of 23.4 bb and 99.5% saw a flop; the rest, 2.5 bb and 39.0%.

So the obvious method — scale each player's observed rake per 100 hands to all their hands — charges every hand at the rate of the biggest pots. Measured, it runs 25% high.

The screen, and the estimator that replaced the shortcut

PokerStars reconciles completely and not one of its 1,107,219 flop hands rakes above 5.0% of the pot, so anything above 6% is missing money wearing a rake's clothes. Everything else is priced by conditional mean in big blinds over (venue, stake, flop, pot size), converted to dollars only at the end. Fitted on half the measured hands and scored against the other half, it lands within 0.7% on every venue.

The recommendation

A retention list sorted by churn risk
is almost exactly the wrong list.

The list that is best at its job and worst at ours

Ranking by risk alone finds 7,554 genuine leavers out of 7,727 calls — 98% precision, far better than the list we recommend. It reaches 0.8% of the money. It is a list of certain departures worth nothing.

Why the product beats either factor

Money alone reaches 63.1% but wastes two thirds of its calls on players who were not going anywhere. Risk × value reaches 70%, and the two lists share 0.1% of their names.

Checked on players the model never saw

Repeated on the held-out fold alone — 19,360 players, 1,936 calls, labels no model ever trained on — the picture is identical: 70.0% of the money against 0.9%, on 0.2% shared names. The recommendation does not depend on players the model had already met.

The caveats travel with it
  • July 2009. Seventeen years old. The method transfers; the magnitude is a micro-stakes market of its time.
  • Rake is derived, never recorded — 34.4% measured, the rest priced from comparable hands.
  • Twenty-six days. This is short-horizon lapse, not churn. Nobody in this data can be shown to have left for good.
  • No ground truth for bots — which is exactly why the supervised question is lapse, and why bot detection is not claimed.
  • Player IDs are per venue. The same human on two sites is two people here, and we never merge them.
