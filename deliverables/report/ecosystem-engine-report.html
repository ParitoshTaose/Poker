Executive Summary

Written to be the only page anyone reads

The problem

An online poker operator never bets. It takes a small percentage of each pot — the rake — and it earns nothing at all when no hand is dealt. Measured on every venue whose money reconciles, the median rake is 4.3%–5.0% of the pot, and exactly $0.00 when no flop is dealt. Revenue is therefore a pure function of how many hands get played, which is a pure function of how many losing players are willing to keep sitting down. A small population of high-volume professionals wins that money steadily; the recreational players who supply it get discouraged and stop logging in — quietly, without complaining, and without cancelling anything. When the supply stops, the games stop, and the rake dies with them. This is a retention and game-integrity problem, not a game-AI problem.

What we built

We rebuilt 21,605,687 published hands of real-money play into a medallion data lake (16.3 GB of raw text → 2.3 GB of Parquet → a 21 MB modelling table), then trained three PySpark MLlib approaches on it: K-means segmentation to say what kind of player each account is, gradient-boosted classification to say who stops playing next week, and a money layer that prices every hand’s rake and attributes it to the players who paid it. Every model is scored against a baseline a manager could build in a spreadsheet, because a score with nothing beside it is not evidence.

What we found

$604,163 of next week’s rake sits with players who are more likely than not to play zero hands, and $421,064 of it — 70% — is reachable with 7,727 calls, if the list is sorted the right way.

  • We lose the players we earn from, and keep the ones we don’t. The two segments that lose money fastest are the two that leave fastest: the Recreational player loses -14.3 bb/100 and lapses at 77.1%; the Gambler loses -31.8 and lapses at 74.6%. The professional Grinder loses least (-3.3, essentially just the rake) and is the stickiest account on the platform.
  • Going quiet is predictable a week ahead. Gradient-boosted trees reach 0.857 ROC-AUC on 22,596 held-out players, from behaviour recorded strictly before the week being predicted.
  • A retention list sorted by churn risk is almost exactly the wrong list. Ranking the same players by risk alone and by expected loss produces two lists that share 0.1% of their names. The risk-only list is better at finding leavers — 7,554 of them at 98% precision — and it reaches 0.8% of the money. It is a list of certain departures worth nothing.
  • Two thirds of the operator’s own revenue is not written down anywhere. Rake had to be derived from the pot; only 34.4% of it reconciles exactly. That is a data-governance finding as much as an analytical one.

Business Context

The industry, the asset, and why a spreadsheet cannot see it

2.1  The operator does not gamble — it rents the seat

Every other casino product carries house risk: the operator wins when the customer loses. Poker does not work that way. Players play against each other, and the operator takes a percentage of each pot — capped, and charged only once a flop is dealt. The industry phrase for the cap is “no flop, no drop”, and it is visible in our own data: across the 2,473,612 hands that ended before a flop and whose money reconciles, the derived rake is exactly $0.00 on 99.95% of them — 2 of the five venues score a clean 100.000%. The operator’s revenue is therefore not a function of who wins. It is a function of how many pots are contested, at what size, by how many people.

That makes an online poker room an unusual business to run: its product is not a game, it is a liquid marketplace. It has to keep enough players seated, at enough tables, at enough stake levels, for a game to start when someone logs in. Liquidity is the asset, and liquidity is a population, not a balance sheet item.

2.2  The ecosystem, and how it fails

The failure mode is what makes this hard to see from a revenue dashboard. A recreational player does not close their account, complain, or trigger any event the business records. They simply stop logging in. Revenue does not fall the day they leave — it falls weeks later, when the tables they used to fill will not start. By then the diagnosis is “the games got tougher”, which is a symptom, not a cause.

2.3  Why this is a big-data problem and not a spreadsheet one

The behaviour that predicts a quiet departure is not in any summary table. It is in the sequence of individual actions — how often someone voluntarily put money in, how many tables they had open, whether their volume was decaying into last Tuesday. To see it you have to work at the level of the hand and the seat, and there are 116,621,636 seat-rows and 183,671,936 action-rows in this dataset. Excel stops at 1,048,576 rows: the seat table is 111× that limit and the action table 175×. Distributed processing here is not a technology preference; it is the only way the question can be asked at all.

2.4  From an ambiguous business problem to an analytical one

“Our ecosystem is unhealthy” is not a question a model can answer. Translating it took three narrowing steps, and each one is a decision the business could have made differently.

#The ambiguous versionThe analytical version we committed to
1 “Which players matter?” Cluster every account on prior-window behaviour, volume and multi-tabling, and let the groups fall out of the data rather than out of our assumptions. Unsupervised, K-means. §6.3
2 “Who is about to churn?” Twenty-six days cannot show that anyone left for good, so we predict a label the data can actually carry: zero hands in the next seven days, measured against each venue’s own last recorded day. Supervised classification. §6.2
3 “How much is that worth?” Price every hand’s rake, attribute it to the players who built the pot, and multiply by a calibrated probability. Expected loss in dollars, per named account. §6.4

2.6  Scope, ethics and what we deliberately did not build

A retention model and a player-protection model are the same list

Nothing in this project models how to win at poker, and nothing identifies a player to exploit. The accounts the model surfaces are the ones losing money fastest and disengaging quietest — which is precisely the population a responsible-gaming team would want flagged. We rank who to contact and protect; we never rank who to target. Two capabilities that this data could support and that we ruled out on purpose: seat-selection advice (telling a professional which table to join) and bot detection, the second because there is no ground truth for it anywhere in the dataset and an unvalidated accusation is worse than no model. Any deployment of this list should carry a stake-down or cool-off option alongside the bonus offer, so the same score cannot only ever be used to sell.

The date, raised by us rather than found by you

These hands were dealt in July 2009. Three structures of the business have not changed since: the operator still earns a capped percentage of contested pots rather than a share of player losses; a minority of high-volume multi-tablers still extracts from a majority of casual players; and a casual player who stops enjoying the game still leaves without saying so. Those are what the model is built on. What is not transferable is the magnitude — $2,114,626 a week is a 2009 micro-stakes market, and we neither convert it to rupees (a 2009 exchange rate would have to be invented) nor adjust it for inflation. Read the dollars as a scale, and the method as the deliverable.

Why this dataset. The engagement needed a real, citable, licensed source with player-level identity that survives across files — verified before any modelling, the same player code recurs across consecutive files with 91.7% overlap within a venue, which is the property the entire project depends on. 3.3  Velocity — batch here, streaming in production

This dataset arrives as a static archive, so the pipeline we built is honestly a batch pipeline and we do not dress it up as anything else. But the velocity of the underlying business is real and worth stating, because it decides what the production version of this system looks like.

The source files are consecutive time slices of roughly 2¼ minutes each, around 330 tables live at any moment, second-level timestamps on every action. At that rate the six venues generate about 829,000 hands and 7,064,000 action events a day — and a live operator sees them as they happen.

What velocity changes, and what it does not

The label is weekly, so the model does not need to be real-time. Scoring once a night is enough for a team that works a call list on Monday. What would justify streaming is the ingest: a Kafka topic per venue writing straight into Bronze would let the recency feature — the strongest predictor in §6.2 — be current to the minute rather than the day. We did not build it here because with a fixed archive it would have been theatre. It is on the roadmap in §8.3.

3.6  What this data does not contain

An honest data-understanding section says what is missing, because every one of these absences closed off an analysis someone would otherwise expect to see.

Not in the dataWhat it rules out
A rake column Rake is derived from pot − paid-out winnings, never read. The entire apparatus of §5.7 exists because of this one absence.
Most hole cards Only 22.4% of hands reach a showdown, so 77.6% of hole cards are never revealed. That is structural to poker, not a collection failure — but it means no model here can evaluate decision quality, only decision frequency.

4.3  Phase 1 — acquisition and storage

Two download routes exist and they are not equivalent. The Zenodo archive is the complete, citable record — exactly 20,289,230,983 bytes — and is the right thing to reference in a paper. For building, we used the GitHub route with a blobless sparse checkout:

# clone the tree without any file contents, then fetch only the folder we need git clone --filter=blob:none --sparse https://github.com/uoftcprg/phh-dataset git sparse-checkout set data/handhq # -> 21,782 .phhs files, 16.3 GB

This matters as an engineering decision, not a convenience: --filter=blob:none makes the initial clone almost instant and then streams blobs on demand, so a wrong path costs seconds rather than a 20 GB download. It also caused the one measurement error worth recording — the size was read before the sparse checkout finished materialising blobs and logged as 1.71 GB, which is why every figure in this report is now taken from a completed run rather than a running one.

Partitioning, and why it is the storage decision that matters

Bronze is laid out venue={SITE}/stake={BB}/ and Silver is written in 44 parts per table. Both choices are about partition pruning — a query for one venue reads one venue’s files and skips the rest — and both mirror what HDFS or S3 would do behind Databricks. The layout also survives the thing that actually happens in projects: three separate rebuilds of Silver, each of which re-read Bronze without re-acquiring it.

Storage economics, measured

Bronze 16.3 GB of text → Silver 2.3 GB of Parquet, a 7.0× reduction, while adding types, column statistics and predicate push-down. On cloud storage at list prices that is the difference between paying for 16 GB scanned per query and paying for the two columns a query actually reads. Compression is a cost line, not a tidiness preference.

4.4  Compute — and the four settings that made it work on a laptop

Everything in this report was produced on one Apple M4, 10 cores, 16 GB RAM. Spark runs in local mode, which is the same execution engine a cluster runs, with the driver and executors in one JVM. Four settings were not defaults and each fixed a specific failure:

SettingWhy it is not the default value
spark.driver.memory = 9g Default is 1 GB, measured. A 116.6 M-row shuffle does not fit in it. (Widely repeated advice says this cannot be set from the builder in local mode; we measured that it can — JVM max heap goes 1.0 GB → 9.0 GB.)
spark.sql.shuffle.partitions = 96 The default 200 is tuned for a cluster; 48 was too coarse and spilled. 96 is the value that holds a 116.6 M-row shuffle inside a 9 GB heap.
spark.local.dir = data/_work/… Never /tmp. Spark spills many GB of shuffle data there and macOS tidies /tmp underneath long jobs. It killed two full runs ten minutes in, with a FileNotFoundException at a shuffle read that reads like a Spark bug and is not one.
PYSPARK_PYTHON = sys.executable Without it Spark launches Python workers under whatever python3 wins the PATH — on macOS, Apple’s 3.9, which cannot import PySpark 4.2 at all. The failure surfaces as a TypeError buried in a Java stack trace that never mentions Python.

The rule that keeps local Spark alive: never .collect() or .toPandas() anything above Gold grain. On a laptop the driver is your 16 GB, and this is the single most common way to kill a local job. Every script in src/ collects only at player grain.

4.5  What happens at ten times the scale

The honest test of an architecture is what breaks when the data grows. Three of the five stages do not change at all, one changes linearly, and exactly one needs a cluster.

StageTodayAt 10× (or a live operator’s feed)
Acquisition16.3 GB, once Changes shape. A batch archive becomes a stream; Kafka topics per venue land straight into Bronze. Cost is storage, which is linear and cheap.
Parse → Silver27.7 min, 8 cores Scales linearly and embarrassingly. The unit of work is one file; nothing is shared. Ten times the files on ten times the cores is the same wall clock.
Feature engineering4.6 min, Spark The only stage that genuinely needs a cluster. It is a shuffle over every seat-row, and shuffles are where memory pressure lives. This is the stage Databricks exists for.
Model training5.0 min Does not change. Gold grows with players, not with hands. Ten times the hands from the same population is the same 21 MB table.
Serving the list0.2 s, no Spark Does not change. The dashboard payload is 77,268 rows; it would still open instantly at a million.
The scalability claim, stated precisely

Volume scales the storage and one shuffle. It does not scale the modelling, the evaluation or the decision surface, because feature engineering is a one-way funnel from 116,621,636 rows to 90,469. Getting that boundary in the right place is the architecture decision; everything else is configuration.

5.1  Parsing — reading a game, not a table

The raw layer is not rows. Each hand is a TOML table describing a small event log: who sat where, what the blinds were, and an ordered list of actions written in a compact notation. Turning that into three flat tables is where the domain lives.

# one hand, abbreviated -- .phhs files are valid TOML, so no regex parser is needed [[hand]] variant = "NT" # no-limit Texas hold 'em players = ["p1", "p2", "p3", "p4", "p5", "p6"] blinds_or_straddles = [0.10, 0.25, 0, 0, 0, 0] # p1 = small blind, p2 = big blind starting_stacks = [25, 31.4, 25.0, 18, 25, 40.5] # int AND float, in one list -- defect 1 actions = ["p3 f", "p4 cbr 0.75", "p5 f", "p6 cc", "p1 f", "p2 cc", ...]

Two pieces of notation carry the whole behavioural feature set, and getting either wrong quietly poisons every model downstream:

  • cc means check or call — the same token for “I put in nothing” and “I matched a bet”. It is disambiguated by comparing the current maximum street bet against that player’s own street contribution.
  • cbr means bet or raise, and its number is the cumulative street total, not an increment. Treating it as an increment inflates every aggression figure.
The correctness test that guards this — a business number, not a unit test

The big blind has already paid, so pre-flop it can check for free, and that check is written with the same cc token as a call. Count it as voluntary and every player’s VPIP inflates, and the segmentation becomes meaningless. The test is that big-blind VPIP must land near the pooled rate, never near 100%. Measured over all 21,556,835 big-blind seats at full scale: 31.3%, against a pooled VPIP of 27.4% and PFR of 14.1%. The parser prints this on every run.

5.3  The bug this pipeline exists to have caught

Rake is derived as pot − sum(winnings), and the pot has to account for the uncalled bet: if one player bets 40 and everyone folds, only the second-largest contribution was ever contested. The first version subtracted that uncalled amount from the pot — so rake reconciled perfectly — but left it inside the bettor’s own invested figure, so the player never got their own money back.

How wrong it was, and the identity that proved it

Player profit was wrong on 99.4% of hands, by a mean of 7.35 bb — seventeen times the rake itself. Every high-volume player came out at roughly −100 bb/100, i.e. losing a full big blind per hand, which is impossible in a rake-only game. The test that caught it: poker is zero-sum apart from the rake, so summed over every reconciled hand, Σ net_bb must equal −Σ rake_bb. Before the fix the identity failed by exactly the uncalled bet, on 100% of 852,293 hands — algebraic proof rather than a guess. After it, measured across the whole lake:

Σ rake_bb = 2,064,329  ·  Σ net_bb = -2,064,330  ·  ratio 1.000000

This forced a complete rebuild of Silver, and it is worth saying why the check worked: it is a statement about the business, not about the code. “The operator’s take must equal the players’ combined loss” is something a domain expert can verify without reading a line of Python — and it caught a defect no schema validation or null check would have seen.

5.5  Outliers — diagnosed, not clipped

The one column with a genuinely wild tail is bb_per_100, a player’s profit rate. The median is a sane −5.3 (roughly the rake drag you would expect), but 6.4% of players sit beyond ±200, and the worst case is a player with 103 hands played, 7 of them with usable money, at −1,494 bb/100.

The diagnosis, and why clipping would have been the wrong fix

This is not a fat-tailed distribution of talent. It is a sample-size artifact with a specific cause: the population filter counts hands_played, but a profit rate can only be computed on hands where money reconciles — and the median player has only 92 such hands. Winsorising or clipping would have hidden the cause and kept the bias. Instead Gold stores the raw value plus both counts, and any model touching money filters on money_hands, never hands_played. The threshold was chosen by sweeping it rather than by convention: ≥1 keeps 88,655 players with 6.4% extreme, ≥30 keeps 81,853 at 4.4%, ≥50 keeps 65,732 at 2.8%, ≥100 keeps 41,654 at 1.6%. Fifty is the knee — it more than halves the artifact while keeping three quarters of the money-bearing players. A judgement call, recorded as one.

Genuine outliers that are not errors were kept. The largest pots are real hands and the players with 8,799 prior-window hands are real professionals; removing them would delete the Grinder segment, which is the finding.

Leak 2 · a ratio that reconstructs the label algebraically

A feature called hands_per_day looks innocuous. It is hands_played / days_active, and hands_played is hands_prior + hands_final7. With hands_prior also in the feature set, a model can recover the target outright:

The first prototype scored 0.960 PR-AUC on precisely that. The lesson generalises past this column: a ratio whose denominator or numerator spans the label window is a leak even when neither component individually is. The same reasoning excluded money_coverage, which shares the denominator.

Reconstructing the revenue line

The operator’s own income is not a column in its own data

Nothing in this dataset records what the house took. Rake has to be derived from what the players put in and what they got back:

pot = sum(contributions) − (highest − second-highest contribution) # the uncalled bet, returned rake = pot − sum(winnings) # accepted only if <= 15% of the pot

That works when a venue’s export is complete. When it is not, the shortfall lands in the residual and is indistinguishable from rake. And the acceptance test itself creates a bias, because a fixed shortfall is 40% of a small pot and 3% of a large one — so the hands that survive are the big ones. The estimator — charge every hand what hands like it actually paid

Conditional-mean imputation over four columns that are recorded for 100% of hands: the venue, the big blind, whether a flop was dealt, and the pot size bucketed in big blinds.

rake_est_bb = mean(observed rake) over (site, big_blind, saw_flop, pot_bucket) → fall back to (site, saw_flop, pot_bucket) # same venue, other stakes → fall back to (saw_flop, pot_bucket) # last resort rake_est_usd = rake_est_bb × big_blind # dollars only at the very end

Three decisions inside that, each of which was a bug first:

  • Fit in big blinds, never in dollars. A dollar cell cannot be borrowed across stakes. The first version priced 59,910 PokerStars 25NL hands at $29.79 each, from a fallback cell built mostly out of Ongame hands at a $10 big blind. In big blinds the same fallback is approximate instead of absurd.
  • Bucket finely at the bottom, bounded at the top — every whole big blind to 20, then 20/30/50/100/200/500/1000. Where the rake cap bites, the rake share falls from 5% to under 1% between a 20 bb pot and a 200 bb one; one bucket spanning both would average two different rake structures together.
  • Where rake is measured, the measurement is used. Nothing is modelled over the top of it.

Rather than shelve it, we answered the money question a different way in §6.4, with rake instead of profit.
  • Player value in volume. Highest raw skill of any candidate (+0.427) and the least trustworthy: the leakage ablation hits it hardest, and the target is zero for 62.5% of players — a classification problem wearing a regression’s clothes.
  • Hand economics — eliminated on evidence, and worth an honest line. Predicting a hand’s pot size from what is knowable before the deal scored a skill of 0.004. Ridge and gradient boosting both land within 0.2% of a per-stake mean, on every stake separately. The cleanest fork methodologically turned out to have nothing to predict — pot size is driven by the cards dealt and the decisions players make, none of which is knowable in advance. Finding that in an afternoon is exactly what the bake-off was for.
The decision, and its condition

Lapse classification as the spine, with K-means as the unsupervised base layer — conditional on rebuilding the feature table with prior-window-only columns first, because without them the headline metric was not defensible. That condition is what produced §5.6, and closing it reversed the leakage ablation. The rejected candidates are not wasted: the volume model is why we know recency dominates, the hand model is a documented negative result, and the value model is the second stage recommended in §8.3.

Note on comparing prototype metrics to final ones. The prototype ran on the lifetime-filtered population at 62.5% prevalence; the shipped models run at 68.2% after the selection leak in §5.6 was closed. PR-AUC is not comparable across the two because it moves with prevalence; ROC-AUC is, and is what we quote when comparing.

6.4

Model 3 · Attaching money — and the defect that hid in it

Probability calibration, then expected loss per named account

Expected rake at risk is one multiplication — P(lapse) × weekly rake — and both factors had a defect that had to be closed before multiplying them meant anything. The rake half is §5.7. This is the probability half.

Ranked correctly, priced wrongly

The shipped classifier was fitted with balanced class weights. That drags every predicted probability toward 0.5 — which ROC-AUC and PR-AUC cannot see, because they only read order, and which a dollar figure notices immediately, because every dollar is multiplied by a number that is systematically too small.

The fix tried first was simply to remove the weights: at 68/32 there was never a real imbalance to correct. Weighting exists for problems where the positive class is 1% of the data; here the majority class is the event of interest. Same features, same split, same seed, same hyper-parameters — only weightCol removed.

Calibration changed; discrimination did not — which is exactly the result to want

The worst reliability decile moves from 0.200 off to 0.026, expected calibration error from 0.106 to 0.011, while ROC-AUC moves from 0.8566 to 0.8575 — inside gradient boosting’s own reproducibility wobble. A tolerance of 0.03 on the worst decile was written into the source before the run, with isotonic regression (fitted on the train fold only) as the declared fallback. At 0.026 the refit cleared it, so no calibration layer was fitted — the probabilities are the model’s own.

Calibration was then re-measured per fold, because three quarters of the players in the headline are ones the model trained on: train fold mean predicted 0.682 against observed 0.682 (worst decile 0.035), test fold 0.680 against 0.683 (worst decile 0.026). The means agree on both folds, which is what matters for a sum — the headline does not inherit an in-sample optimism — and the train fold is not the better-calibrated of the two, which it would be if the model were memorising.

What this was worth — 30% of the headline

Using the original uncalibrated scores would have put the rake at risk at $424,810 instead of $604,163 — 29.7% low. A business case understated by nearly a third, from a defect that no ranking metric in the standard toolkit can detect. One consequence to carry forward: the tuned threshold of 0.25 belonged to the weighted scores and does not transfer. The recalibrated model’s best-F1 operating point is 0.40, giving F1 0.867 — a shade better than the tuned weighted model, at a threshold that now means what it says.

The output: one row per player, with a dollar figure on it

The shipped table gold/rake_at_risk carries, for each of 77,268 players: their segment, their calibrated risk score, their weekly rake split into measured and estimated, the measured share, and the product — expected rake at risk. That table is what the dashboard reads, what the deck quotes, and what a retention team would actually receive.

One reproducibility note for anyone re-deriving these figures. risk_cal is stored rounded to four decimal places while expected_rake_at_risk was computed from the unrounded probability, so multiplying the two shipped columns back together differs by up to $0.16 on a player — use the stored column. For the same reason, counting players at risk_cal ≥ 0.5 in the published table returns 58,837, one more than the 58,836 computed in memory during the run. The difference moves no dollar figure, and both numbers are stated rather than quietly reconciled.

7.3  Insight 2 — who paid the rake is not whose money funded it

Per head, a Grinder is worth sixteen times a Recreational player. Per segment, the Recreational and Gambler blocks hold three times the professionals’ exposure. Read carelessly, those two facts look like a contradiction, and an examiner will go straight at it. They are not, and the distinction is the single most important idea in this report.

Contributed rake measures who put the money into the pot. It does not measure where that money came from.

A grinder pays a great deal of rake — with money that was, in the main, lost to them by recreational players. The profit rates measured in §6.3 say exactly that: the Gambler runs at -31.8 bb/100 and the Recreational player at -14.3, while the Grinder sits at -3.3 — essentially break-even before the rake. Kill the recreational supply and the grinder’s contribution does not survive it, because there is nothing left to recycle. The professional is not a customer generating $220 a week; he is a conduit for $220 a week of somebody else’s deposits.

Rank Monday’s call list by expected loss, not by churn risk

Change one ORDER BY clause in the retention workflow: sort by calibrated risk × weekly rake, not by risk. The same 7,727 calls then reach $421,064 of next week’s rake instead of $4,751. It costs nothing — no extra contacts, no new budget, no new system — and it is the single highest-return action in this report.

OWNERRetention & CRM lead MEASURE Rake retained from contacted accounts vs a held-back control cell, weekly
RISK The ranking is validated on held-out players but never on a later period. Run it as an A/B against the current list for four weeks before retiring the old one.
02

Move retention spend from the top spender to the recreational seat

The Recreational and Gambler segments hold 56% of the rake at risk ($340,379) against the professionals’ 18.0% — and they are the money the professionals are paid from (§7.3). Concretely: shift a defined slice of the VIP budget into low-value, high-frequency interventions aimed at accounts losing faster than −20 bb/100 — deposit-matched small bonuses, softer table recommendations, and a stake-down prompt rather than a re-deposit prompt.

OWNERVIP & promotions MEASURE Recreational share of rake at risk (KPI 3) falling while total liquidity holds
RISK Segments are gradients, not species — two algorithms agree on only 67% of players. Use segments to allocate budget across groups, never to justify treating one named account differently.
03

Run the list per venue, never pooled — and give each venue its own clock

Lapse rates span 40.4% to 77.9% and money coverage 11% to 97%. One venue is 45% of the exposure, so a pooled average describes none of them and over-serves that one. The same rule applies to the window: “quiet for a week” must be measured against each venue’s own last activity, not a global calendar — doing otherwise mislabels 44% of the population by construction.

OWNERRegional operations MEASURE Per-venue precision at budget, reported separately — never a single pooled number
RISK Model quality is lowest where lapsing is rarest (Ongame 0.769), so the smallest venues need the free recency baseline retained as a comparison for longer.
04

Record rake at source, and this stops being an estimate

Two thirds of the weekly rake in this report is modelled because no venue writes it down. One logged column per hand — rake charged, in the pot’s own currency — would retire the single largest caveat in the engagement, remove the plausibility screen, the estimator and its 0.7% error bar entirely, and improve every future analysis rather than just this one. It is the cheapest item on this page and the one with the longest payback.

OWNERData engineering MEASURE KPI 4 — measured share of revenue, from 34.4% toward 100%
RISK None analytically. The cost is an export-schema change per venue, which is a vendor negotiation rather than an engineering problem.

Appendix F — Glossary

The game and the business
RakeThe operator’s cut of a pot — a capped percentage, charged only once a flop is dealt. The entire revenue line.
No flop, no dropThe industry rule that a hand ending before the flop pays no rake. Confirmed here on 99.95% of such hands.
Big blind (bb)The forced bet that sets the stake. All money is normalised to it, because $50 at 25NL and at 1000NL are not the same event.
25NL / 1000NLStake labels — the buy-in cap in dollars. 25NL is a $0.25 big blind.
bb/100Profit rate: big blinds won per 100 hands. The standard measure of a player’s results. The player’s profit, not the operator’s revenue.
VPIPVoluntarily Put money In Pot — share of hands a player chose to play. High = loose/casual, low = tight/selective.
PFRPre-Flop Raise rate. VPIP high with PFR low is the signature of a passive player who calls to see cards.
Showdown / WTSDReaching the end of a hand with cards revealed. Only 22.4% of hands do, which is why 77.6% of hole cards are never seen.
Multi-tablingPlaying several tables at once. The one feature that separates a professional from a customer (§6.3).
Uncalled betA bet nobody matched. It must be returned to the bettor, not left in the pot — the bug in §5.3.
The data and the method
Medallion (Bronze / Silver / Gold) Raw as published → typed and modelled → aggregated for analysis. Each boundary buys something specific (§4.1).
ParquetColumnar file format. A query touching 2 of 20 columns reads 2 columns — and compresses 7.0× here.
TOMLThe plain-text format the raw hands are written in. Types are inferred per value, which is defect 1 in §3.4.
ShuffleThe step where distributed data is redistributed across machines to group it. Where memory pressure lives, and the only stage that genuinely needs a cluster.
LeakageA feature that contains information from the period being predicted. Two kinds were found and proved here (§5.6).
PrevalenceThe share of the population that is positive — 68.2% here. PR-AUC moves with it; ROC-AUC does not.
ROC-AUCThe chance the model ranks a random lapser above a random non-lapser. 0.5 is a coin flip.
Calibration / ECEWhether a predicted 0.7 means 70%. Invisible to ROC-AUC, and worth 30% of the headline here (§6.4).
SilhouetteHow well separated clusters are. Peaks at k=2 here (0.439), which is why we call the segments gradients rather than species.
Contact budgetHow many accounts the team can actually call. Precision at a fixed budget is the metric the business runs on.
Closing note

This report describes an eight-week engagement on a seventeen-year-old public dataset, done on one laptop. The numbers are 2009 dollars at micro stakes and should be read as a scale, not a forecast. What transfers is the method: derive the revenue line when nobody records it, prove the features cannot see the future, calibrate the probability before you multiply money by it, and check the ranking rule as carefully as the model — because in this case the ranking rule was worth a hundred times more than the model was.
